\subsection{Reaction--diffusion systems}
\label{sec:rd-systems}

% Brief explanation of Reaction Diffusion Systems
A reaction diffusion system is a \textit{Nonlinear Dynamical system} that models how two or more chemicals \textit{react} and \textit{diffuse} in space and time. A reaction of two chemicals results in new substances being formed while diffusion occurs when a chemical spreads from a region with lower concentration to higher concentration. [Citation Needed].
General Form of a Reaction Diffusion System:
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:rd-general}
	\pd{\mathbf{q}}{t} = D \nabla \mathbf{q} + R(\mathbf{q}),
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ is the vector of concentrations for each chemical at position $\mathbf{x}$
% Explanation of the system
and time $t$, $R(\mathbf{q})$ is the reaction component of the sytem , and $D$ is a matrix representing coefficient of diffusions. The Laplacian $\nabla$ compares the concentration of chemicals around a neighbourhood in space and pushes the molecules of a chemical to lower concentration.

% Components of a Reaction Diffusion System
Reaction Diffusion Systems can be of multiple components. Components are defined by how many spatial dimensions are used. For instance, a single component RD systems only models the phenonmenon against one space dimension and time. Two component models are needed for producing pattenrns as shown by turing [citaion needed]. There are also many kinds of reaction diffusion system models. In this report, we will be focusing on a simple RD System named the Gray-Scott Model. (\cref{sec:gray-scott}).

\subsection{Turing patterns}
\label{sec:turing-patterns}
\textcite{turing1952} asked how an embryo, which starts out almost uniform, develops
regular structures. His answer was surprising: although diffusion normally smooths
things out, if two chemicals diffuse at \emph{different} rates, diffusion can make a
uniform state unstable, so that small random disturbances grow into a regular pattern.
This is called a \emph{diffusion-driven} or \emph{Turing instability}, and the resulting
patterns are called \emph{Turing patterns}. The intuition is short-range activation and
long-range inhibition: one substance promotes its own production locally, while the
faster-spreading effect of the other suppresses it further away, so peaks form with gaps
between them. Reaction--diffusion is now a widely used framework for explaining
biological patterns such as animal markings \parencite{kondo2010}.

% How turing patterns are connected to computer graphics
The idea entered computer graphics in 1991. \textcite{turk1991} simulated
reaction--diffusion directly on the surface of a 3D model, so the texture fits the shape
without distortion, and combined several systems to produce leopard rosettes and giraffe
patches. \textcite{witkin1991} extended the method with direction-dependent and
spatially varying diffusion. Both show that reaction--diffusion is a practical texture
technique, not just a mathematical curiosity.

\subsection{The Gray--Scott model}
\label{sec:gray-scott}
The Gray--Scott model was introduced as a model of an autocatalytic chemical reaction [citaiton needed here].
in a continuously fed, well-mixed tank \parencite{grayscott1984}. The chemical rule proposed in the gray scott model is a simplifciation and not an accurate one.
\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gs-reactions}
	U + 2V \to 3V, \qquad V \to P,
\end{equation}

% My interpretation of this model
\begin{itemize}
	\item U reacts 2 molecules of V to produce 3V.
	\item Therefore, the model is similar to a prey-predator model?
	\item V also get killed by kill rate.
	\item U is constantly fed into a well mixed tank.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:gray-scott}
	\begin{aligned}
		\pd{U}{t} & = \Du \lap U - UV^2 + \GSF(1 - U),    \\
		\pd{V}{t} & = \Dv \lap V + UV^2 - (\GSF + \GSk)V.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


The term $UV^2$ is the rate of the first reaction. It is removed from $U$ and added to
$V$, $V$ consumes $U$ to produce another one of itself. The term
$\GSF(1 - U)$ is the \emph{feed}: how much $U$ is added to the container, pushing $U$ back
towards its reservoir value of $1$.  The equation is dimensionless.
